The three things that bother me most about AI are not the ones that get the most attention.

Not the extinction scenarios. Not the deepfakes. Not the possibility that superintelligence will arrive before we understand ordinary intelligence.

What actually bothers me is something quieter: that we are building a system we cannot fully evaluate, staffed by people whose capacity to evaluate it is eroding exactly as the system becomes more powerful.

---

Ethan Mollick spent time studying what happens when AI is very good at its job. His finding is counterintuitive: the better the AI performs, the worse the human oversight becomes. When a tool gives you a high-quality first answer, you stop working hard to verify it. You stop paying close attention. The output looks right, reads well, contains no obvious errors — so you accept it and move on.

This is not laziness. It is a predictable human response to a reliable tool. We calibrate our attention to the apparent quality of what we're checking. The problem is that AI errors are not obvious.

They are not wild fabrications — those are easy to catch. They are subtle, plausible-sounding mistakes: a statistic off by a year, a medical term used almost correctly, a code assumption that will work in most cases but fail in the specific one you care about. These errors require domain expertise to detect. And domain expertise is not something AI can check for — it is something the human in the loop is supposed to supply.

That's where the problem starts.

---

The second part is structural. The pipeline that creates experts — the apprenticeship system, informal and invisible — is exactly what AI automation disrupts first. As AI takes on routine tasks, juniors observe rather than do. The surgical resident watching a robotic procedure rather than operating. The junior analyst reviewing AI output rather than building the underlying model. The junior developer accepting AI-generated code rather than writing and failing through their own. The ticket gets closed. The skill doesn't.

Mollick frames this as a pipeline paradox: experts are the only people who can reliably evaluate AI's output, but the pipeline that creates experts is being hollowed out as AI takes over the tasks through which expertise develops. Fewer juniors become seniors. Fewer seniors become experts. The system becomes more dependent on AI precisely as it becomes less equipped to audit it.

---

And then there is the hallucination dial.

The same randomness that makes AI useful for creative work — the loose associative leaps, the willingness to combine disparate ideas — also makes it produce confident-sounding wrong answers. There is no version of the creative, generative AI that is also a reliably factual one. The dial cannot be turned in both directions at once. The more you tune for creativity and fluency, the more you introduce the plausible-error risk.

What bothers me is the direction of it all — an AI system that is getting more capable and a human oversight layer that is getting thinner.

This isn't an argument against AI. It's an argument for paying attention to what erodes quietly while you're busy moving fast.

I keep coming back to the same question: in your domain, who is the audit layer? Who catches the plausible wrong answer? And what are you doing to make sure that person still exists in five years?
