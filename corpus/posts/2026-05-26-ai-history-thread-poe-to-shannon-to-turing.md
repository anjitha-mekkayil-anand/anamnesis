Edgar Allan Poe was thinking about AI in the 1830s.

He saw the Mechanical Turk — a chess-playing automaton that had beaten Napoleon. He published an essay arguing it must be controlled by a hidden human. He was right. The fraud argument was the surface. Underneath it: no machine could ever truly think.

1950. Claude Shannon built Theseus — a mechanical mouse that could learn a maze, remember the solution, and navigate it again. The first machine learning demonstration.

That same year, Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." Opening line: "I propose to consider the question, can machines think?" He didn't answer it. He gave us a test instead — and now we're here.

We talk about AI as though it appeared from nowhere in 2023. It didn't. We've been asking this question for nearly 200 years. We're not at the beginning of something new. We're at a new chapter of a very old question.

Turing refused to answer his own question. 75 years on — do you think we've answered it?
