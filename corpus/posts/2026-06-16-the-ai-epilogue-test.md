I asked Claude to write an epilogue for a book.

The book was Mollick's *Co-Intelligence*. I was experimenting — could I use AI to extend an author's voice, continue their argument? I gave it the final chapter. I asked it to write what might come next.

It pushed back.

It said something like: the book's value is in the specific claim that we're at a genuine inflection point — and an epilogue written now, by AI, would undermine that claim. It suggested the more honest thing was to sit with the uncertainty the author had earned, rather than resolve it artificially.

I expected an output. I got an argument — and the argument was right.

This stuck with me more than most things I've read about AI this year. I'm not making a claim about consciousness or feelings — whatever was happening there is unresolved. It demonstrated something I hadn't fully internalised: prompting for an output and having a conversation with a co-intelligence are different things. The second one can tell you something the first one can't.

Mollick's whole thesis is that AI works best as a thinking partner, not a content machine. That session was the clearest demonstration I've had of why the distinction matters.

Have you ever asked AI for an output and got an argument instead?

(I tried it again this week. This time it argued first, then wrote it. The argument was still the part worth having.)
